# Itten: contrast, form, accent, rhythm

Reference for steps 1, 2, 5, 8, 10 and 11 of `SKILL.md`. Source: Johannes Itten, *Design and Form: The Basic Course at the Bauhaus and Later*, revised edition (1975). Page numbers are the printed pages of that edition. Anything marked *heuristic* is this skill's conversion, not Itten's text.

## Itten's full contrast list (p.12)

These are Itten's pairs, in his order. The right-hand column is a *heuristic* mapping to digital products.

| Pair | Digital product |
|---|---|
| large–small | type scale, card hierarchy, icon sizes |
| long–short | line length, bar length in charts, list item length |
| broad–narrow | column widths, sidebar vs. main, condensed vs. regular type |
| thick–thin | stroke weight, rule weight, font weight |
| black–white (light–dark) | tonal ramp, surfaces, elevation, dark mode |
| much–little | data density vs. breathing room |
| straight–curved | corner radius, line charts vs. step charts |
| pointed–blunt | corner radius, icon terminals, arrow heads |
| horizontal–vertical | reading direction vs. column rhythm |
| diagonal–circular | tension (trend, error) vs. continuity (progress, avatar) |
| high–low | vertical placement, z-order, header vs. footer |
| area–line | filled surface vs. border, divider, outline icon |
| area–body | flat surface vs. shadow, depth |
| line–body | outline vs. elevated object |
| smooth–rough | flat fill vs. grain, noise, texture |
| hard–soft | edge sharpness, shadow blur, focus rings |
| still–moving | static state vs. transition and animation |
| light–heavy | font weight, color density |
| transparent–opaque | overlay, scrim, blur, modal layer |
| continuous–intermittent | solid vs. dashed, determinate vs. indeterminate progress |
| liquid–solid | fluid vs. fixed layout, elastic vs. rigid motion |
| sweet–sour | (expressive only: tone of voice, palette timbre) |
| strong–weak | emphasis vs. de-emphasis in type and color |
| loud–soft | (expressive only: sound, motion intensity) |

The seven color contrasts come on top of these (p.32–33). See `color.md`.

His illustrated plates (p.10–11) add point, line, plane and volume, and "contrasts in directions".

**How Itten taught a contrast** (p.12): students had to experience each contrast with the senses, then grasp it intellectually, then make it. He placed the life and beauty of the contrast world in the **gradations** between the poles. Treat each declared contrast as a scale, and place elements on it deliberately.

**Choosing:** the chosen contrast must produce a definite expression (p.16). Combining two or more form characters or contrasts takes a strong feeling for form, to arrange the forces as a balanced whole (p.72). The skill's "one dominant contrast, two subordinate at most" is a *heuristic* that enforces this.

## Light and dark (p.16–30)

- **First exercise:** a white circle outlined on white paper only reads once a tone surrounds it, and a light, delicate gray is enough (p.16, illustration p.18).
- **Never begin in outline.** Light–dark compositions start from patches and masses, whose extent is set by the intensity of light and dark (p.16; the p.26 caption repeats it).
- **Tone value scale:** single-tone steps, with no white or black lines between them. Gifted students reached up to 44 steps from white to black (p.16–17).
- **Light–dark chords** can be built from areas of the same size or of different proportions (p.16).
- **Different sizes need different strengths.** Areas of different size need tones of different strength (p.16). The exercise "areas of various sizes and tone values balanced out" is on p.20. A large dark form becomes more significant when a small bright form counteracts it (p.16). Itten does not say which way round: "large = quiet, small = strong" is *heuristic*.
- **Reversal:** in a light scheme the dark tones stand out; in a dark scheme the light ones do (p.16, shown p.26).
- **Goya exercise:** a painting is squared off and translated into blacks, whites and grays only, with no objects, so the student studies the whole picture area (p.21). Nature studies are first translated into light–dark values (p.22).
- **Identical tones attract; different tones repel** (p.30, "The Jumping Dog").

## Form character (p.62–72)

- **The three basic forms are typified by direction** (p.62):
  - Square: horizontal and vertical. Its constant repetition of right angles produces a tense movement from corner to corner.
  - Triangle: diagonal. All angles are represented in it.
  - Circle: an evenly curved, continuously moving line. Students first swung it with the whole body, then felt it without moving, then drew it.
- **This book gives no colors for the three forms.** The square-red / triangle-yellow / circle-blue mapping belongs to Itten's color books, such as *The Art of Color* (1961), and to Kandinsky's 1923 Bauhaus questionnaire, not to *Design and Form*. Do not cite this book for it.
- **Unified form character:**
  - With a team of students, Itten set out to design a room in one form character (p.65).
  - The door with wood relief, with lock metalwork by the student Naum Slutzky, Weimar 1921, was developed from the square and cube (p.65; plate p.66).
  - That lock was among the first workshop tasks of students coming from the Basic Course (p.9).
  - Students also wrote letters in the character of a form, to build a feeling for the regularity of that character (p.68).
- **Motif and variation:**
  - Four matches combined by shifting, rotating, reflecting, glide reflection, inversion, intersection, and changes of proportion, lightness and color give a very large family of forms (p.62).
  - A line motif made of circular, square and triangular elements is varied by inversion, broad or narrow disposition, opposed movement and intersection (p.80).
  - The aim is to find the simplest and clearest form (p.82).
  - Product use: icon families and component variants, built from one motif, an explicit list of allowed transformations, systematic generation, then selection.
- **Positive–negative forms:**
  - The square, rectangle, rhombus, triangle and hexagon with equal sides divide a plane without gaps.
  - Adding to one side what is taken from the other produces congruent interlocking forms (p.82).
  - The chessboard is the perfect division of a plane by one form (p.63).
  - The plates are textile designs, Krefeld 1934 (p.83).
  - Product use: patterns, tile and mosaic layouts, interlocking shapes.

## Proportion (p.62, 78)

- **Named but not developed:** the series 1:2:4:8:16:32 and 1:3:9:27:81, the golden section and the harmonic triangle are all named, but no use is given for them (p.62). Scales of proportion from broad to narrow, in parallel and opposite progressions, are on p.78.
- **Perceived proportion departs from measured proportion.** A long form beside a short one looks longer. Proportion can only be judged by perception, and Itten uses the distortion for an "unreal" dynamism (p.62).
- **The elephant exercise:** a small keeper makes the elephant look large (p.62).

## Accent points and visual paths (p.63, 90–93)

- **The distribution of accents largely decides the effect.** Accents create tension and lead the eye from one to the next. The lines joining them are visual paths. Points on the axes or diagonals of a form are effective (p.63).
- **One main accent** leads the viewer into still contemplation, and the eye keeps returning to it.
- **Several accents of equal strength** make the eye move between them, which creates an experience of movement (p.63). Both are legitimate expressive choices.
- **Plates:**
  - p.90: accent points of different sizes, joined by a network of reference lines.
  - p.91: scattered accents ("The Meeting").
  - p.92: a still life reduced to lines and accents.
  - p.93: "The Class", a distribution of heads in space.
- **Build constructions from the first sketches.** They help find the final format and give order to the sketch's initially random features (p.63).

## Rhythm (p.98–106)

- **Two kinds of rhythm:** regular repetition like a musical beat, or irregular, continuous, free and flowing movement. The sense of rhythm need not be confined to mechanical repetition (p.98).
- **Interruption is costly.** When a march or waltz was stopped and restarted at irregular intervals, students found it almost painful (p.98).
- **Intermediate forms.** Forms develop from one another through transitional forms, so that the rhythm is not broken (p.98, 106).
- **Rhythm without inner dynamism** looks cold, discontinuous and hostile (p.98, about writing).
- **Product translation** (*heuristic*):
  - Spatial rhythm is mostly mechanical.
  - Breaks are few and carry meaning.
  - State changes go through intermediate forms such as transitions and skeletons.

## Texture (p.34–39)

- **Texture contrasts:** smooth–rough, dull–shiny, transparent–opaque (p.37).
- **Texture is secondary.** In the spatial studies the main effect lies in the formal contrasts; differentiation by texture is only secondary (p.39).
- **Product translation:** grain, glass and noise never carry hierarchy.

## Teaching method worth borrowing

- **Free perception before construction** (p.16). The sequence is personal observation, then intellectual explanation, then realization (p.12).
- **The wording of the task shapes the result.** Given "The Tiger", students would draw decorative stripes; given "The Roaring Tiger", fear-inspiring postures (p.125).
- **Three readings, then a synthesis.** A subject is rendered expressively, constructively and impressionistically, and then a generally valid representation is sought (p.132–133). Subjective taste alone is not enough for an objective color judgment (p.33).
- **Analysis of masters, one principle at a time.**
  - After each principle, students analyzed how different masters solved it, grasping the overall composition rather than every detail (p.13, 109–110).
  - Without watchful self-criticism, this produces only academic copying.
- **Expressive color themes.** Night, Christening, Funeral, Fairground and the Seasons, to find each theme's timbre (p.33).
- **The chessboard.** Since 1917, color harmonies were studied on a chessboard, free of form associations (p.32).

## What does not transfer

- Breathing, relaxation and concentration exercises, and their philosophical framing (p.9, 12, 98).
- Expressive brush drawing, memory drawing and "shorthand" forms (p.110–126), except as a habit of internalizing a form before rendering it.
- Material collages, 3D montage and weaving (p.34–61, 84–85), beyond the subordinate texture point.
- **Subjective forms linked to physical appearance and temperament** (p.132). Never turn this into a user or persona typology.
- The golden section and the harmonic triangle, which the book names without giving any method.

## History, as the book gives it

- **Teaching dates.** Itten taught from 1917 to 1960, from his Vienna school (1917–1919) and the Bauhaus Weimar (1919–1923) to the Zurich textile school (1942–1960) (p.15).
- **Leaving the Bauhaus.** In 1923 Gropius said he could no longer answer for Itten's teaching methods, and Itten decided on his own to leave (p.13). He did not believe that the slogans "Back to the Crafts" and "Unity of Art and Technology" could solve the problems of the time (p.9).
- **What the book does not mention.** "Form follows function" and Hannes Meyer do not appear in this book.
- **Who the course was for.** Its texture studies were of value to future architects, craftsmen, photographers, commercial artists and industrial designers. Itten also gave texture exercises to textile merchants, technologists and managers (p.34).
